The Supreme Revolutionary Council: its clan composition, internal power struggles, and the political transformation under Mohamed Siad Barre.

Introduction: A Revolution That Changed Somalia’s History

On 21 October 1969, Somalia entered a new and defining chapter in its political history. Following the assassination of President Abdirashid Ali Shermarke, the country's armed forces took control of the government and announced the establishment of the Supreme Revolutionary Council (SRC). This marked the beginning of a military administration that would govern Somalia for more than two decades.

The revolution brought together a group of military officers from different Somali clans and sub-clans. They became the founding members of a new political and military authority that promised to restore order, eliminate corruption, strengthen national unity, and transform the country.

However, behind the revolutionary slogans and promises lay a complex political reality. The composition of the Council, the distribution of positions, and the relationships among its members gradually became important factors in determining the direction of the new government.

Some members remained influential, while others were dismissed, marginalized, or executed following internal disagreements and political conflicts. Over time, the collective leadership that initially characterized the Council gradually gave way to a more centralized system of authority under Mohamed Siad Barre.

Understanding who these men were, where they came from, and what happened to them is essential to understanding the early history of the Somali military government and the political developments that eventually shaped the country's future.

Internal Disagreements and the Dismissal of Council Members

The Supreme Revolutionary Council did not remain united or unchanged throughout its existence. As the new government consolidated its authority, disagreements emerged among its members. Differences over political direction, personal relationships, and the distribution of influence contributed to tensions within the leadership.

According to the historical account presented here, four individuals were dismissed from the Council.

Jama Ali Qorshil – Darod, Warsangeli

He was reportedly dismissed following accusations of rebellion against the Council.

Ahmed Mahmoud Adde – Hawiye, Abgaal

His dismissal followed an unusual confrontation during a Council meeting.

According to the account, Ahmed Mahmoud Adde arrived late for a meeting and found the door closed. He reportedly forced the door open and entered without authorization. A confrontation then developed between him and Ali Matten Hashi, who allegedly slapped him. The incident ended with Ahmed Mahmoud Adde being removed from the meeting and subsequently dismissed from the Council. Although the incident may appear personal, it illustrates how internal relationships, discipline, and disagreements could have serious consequences within the revolutionary leadership.

Mohamed Ali Shire – Darod, Dhulbahante

His dismissal was reportedly connected to his opposition to what was described as Darod-oriented political policies.

Bashir Qoslaye – Hawiye, Habar Gidir

According to the account, he was dismissed because he was considered unable to meet the standards of discipline and responsibility expected of a member of the Council.

These dismissals reveal that the revolutionary leadership was not a permanently united group. Behind the public image of a disciplined military administration, internal disagreements and struggles over authority were already taking place.

Executions and the Escalation of Internal Power Struggles

The internal conflicts within the Supreme Revolutionary Council reportedly went beyond disagreements and dismissals. According to the information presented, some individuals associated with the revolutionary leadership were sentenced to death and executed. Two names appear in the historical account.

Salad Gubeyri – Hawiye, Abgaal

His execution is associated in the account with disagreements over the distribution of positions and an alleged attempt to organize a counter-coup against the original military coup.

Mohamed Aynashe – Isaaq

His name is also included among those reportedly executed. However, the available account does not provide specific information about the circumstances or reasons behind his execution. These events raise important questions about the internal dynamics of the revolutionary government.

They suggest that the leadership experienced serious divisions and that political disagreements could eventually lead to the removal, punishment, or elimination of individuals who had once been part of the same revolutionary movement. They also illustrate a broader historical lesson: a movement that comes to power through collective action can gradually lose its unity when political authority becomes concentrated and internal differences are no longer managed through consultation and institutional processes.

The Rise of Siad Barre and the Decline of Collective Leadership

Perhaps the most significant political development following the 1969 revolution was the gradual transformation of the Supreme Revolutionary Council from a collective military leadership into a system increasingly dominated by one individual. In the early days of the revolution, authority was formally exercised through the Council, which brought together several military officers who had participated in overthrowing the civilian government. However, as time passed, Mohamed Siad Barre increasingly consolidated his position within the government. The balance of influence among the original revolutionary leaders gradually changed, and the Council's collective role became less prominent. According to the political interpretation presented in this article, Siad Barre increasingly relied on individuals within his own clan-based circle and strengthened their presence in senior government and security positions. At the same time, concerns grew about the declining influence and representation of other Somali communities in important decision-making institutions. This development created a growing gap between the original promises of national unity and equality, and the political realities experienced by different sections of Somali society. For many observers and critics of the regime, this was one of the turning points in the transformation of the revolution from a collective military movement into a highly centralized political system.

The Establishment of the Somali Revolutionary Socialist Party

In 1976, the Somali Revolutionary Socialist Party was established, marking another major turning point in the country's political development. The establishment of the party provided the military government with a formal political framework through which it could organize and administer the state. It also strengthened the central role of Mohamed Siad Barre in the country's political system. Over time, the government became increasingly centralized around the presidency and the ruling party. Independent political activity and meaningful political competition became more restricted. Although the government continued to promote national unity, socialism, and revolutionary ideals, critics increasingly questioned the extent to which these principles were reflected in the actual exercise of political power. Political opponents faced growing restrictions, while allegations of repression, arbitrary detention, exclusion, and violations of human rights became increasingly prominent. The accumulation of political grievances, economic difficulties, social tensions, and armed conflicts gradually weakened the relationship between the government and significant sections of the population.

Growing Opposition and the Fall of the Military Government

The political challenges facing the military government became increasingly difficult to manage as the years passed. Opposition movements expanded, public dissatisfaction grew, and the country experienced mounting political and security instability. The eventual collapse of the regime in January 1991 was not the result of a single event. Rather, it reflected years of accumulated political, economic, social, and institutional problems. Among the major factors contributing to the collapse were:

  1. The excessive concentration of political and military power in the hands of one individual.

  2. The weakening of collective leadership and independent political participation.

  3. Growing allegations of clan-based favoritism and discrimination in the distribution of government positions.

  4. Human rights violations and political and security repression.

  5. Economic hardship and deteriorating social conditions.

  6. The expansion of armed opposition movements and prolonged internal conflict.

  7. The weakening of national institutions and the erosion of public confidence in the government.

Eventually, Mohamed Siad Barre's government collapsed in January 1991. He left Somalia and went into exile, eventually dying in Nigeria in January 1995. His departure brought an end to more than two decades of military rule. However, the political and institutional consequences of that period continued to affect Somalia long after the regime had fallen. The legacy of the military government remains a subject of historical debate. For some, the period is remembered for state-building initiatives and national development achievements, while for others, it is associated with political repression, clan-based grievances, conflict, and the eventual collapse of the Somali state. A balanced understanding of this period requires acknowledging the complexity of its history and examining both its achievements and its failures.

Conclusion: What Somalia Can Learn from the 1969 Revolution

The history of the men who participated in the 21 October 1969 Revolution is more than a record of names, military positions, and clan affiliations. It is a story about power, political ambition, collective leadership, internal disagreements, and the consequences of concentrating authority in the hands of a few individuals. The revolution began with promises of reform, national unity, and the elimination of corruption. Yet, over time, the political system evolved in a direction that increasingly weakened collective leadership, limited political participation, and undermined institutional accountability. The experience offers an important lesson for present and future generations of Somalis: changing a government is not the same as building a successful state.

A genuine national transformation must be founded on justice, equal citizenship, accountability, respect for human rights, and the fair participation of all communities in public affairs. Somalia's diversity should be a source of national strength rather than a reason for political competition, exclusion, or division. Clan identity is part of the country's social history, but it should never determine who deserves political authority, public opportunities, or access to national resources. The future of Somalia depends on building institutions that serve all citizens equally, regardless of their clan, region, background, or political affiliation. The lessons of 1969 remain relevant today. They remind us that political power must be exercised as a public trust, not as a personal possession or a clan privilege.

Somalia belongs to all Somalis. Its unity must be built on justice, its leadership must be guided by accountability, and its future must be secured through equality, mutual respect, and national solidarity.
